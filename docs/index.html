editha

Turn pictures of handwritten and typed labels into a spreadsheet you can search, then check the result before you rely on it.

editha is made for museum specimen labels and archive notes. You do not need to write any code. It works with one image per label, and it can also cut labels out of a photo of a whole specimen first.

What it does, step by step

editha is a short chain of tools. Each one takes the result of the one before. You can stop after any step.

  1. STEP 0 · OPTIONAL

    Cut out the labels (object detection and cropping)

    For photos of a whole specimen. The tool finds each label in the photo and saves it as its own picture.

    Runs on your computer. Free.
  2. STEP 1

    Type up the text (transcription, also called OCR)

    An AI model reads each label picture and writes down exactly what it says, with no tidying up.

    Runs in your browser. Pay per use.
  3. STEP 2

    Sort the text into columns (data structuring)

    The text is split into date, place, collector and so on, using the standard column names for biodiversity data (Darwin Core).

    Runs in your browser. Pay per use.
  4. STEP 3

    Check the work (manual checks and re-reads)

    You compare values with the label picture. The AI reads again as a second opinion, and every disagreement is flagged for you.

    Runs in your browser. Pay per use.

What you end up with: a corrected spreadsheet (CSV file), plus a short report with how often the readings agreed, ready for a methods section.

Where do I start?

I have…

Photos of whole specimens

Each photo shows the specimen with one or more labels pinned beneath it.

Start with the label finder to cut the labels out, then go on to the label reader.

I have…

One picture per label, or scans of pages

Individual label images, notebook pages or ledger scans.

Skip the label finder. Go straight to the label reader.

The two tools

They are kept separate on purpose, because they work very differently.

Step 0

Label finder

  • Finds labels in a photo and crops them (YOLO object detection).
  • Needs Python, which a double-click installer sets up for you.
  • Free to run. Your pictures never leave your computer.
  • You can teach it a new kind of label with a small number of examples you draw yourself (annotation and training).

Open the label finder guide

Steps 1 to 3

Label reader

  • Three web pages you open in your browser: type up, sort, check.
  • Nothing to install.
  • Needs an account with OpenAI or Anthropic and a key to use it (API key). They charge per picture.
  • Every setting, prompt and count can be exported for your methods section.

Open the label reader guide

Your pictures and your privacy

  • The label finder works entirely on your computer.
  • The label reader sends each picture from your browser straight to the AI company you choose (OpenAI or Anthropic). It goes nowhere else. There is no editha server.
  • Results, prompts and settings are kept in your browser until you press Start over. They are not uploaded anywhere. Download your files before clearing anything.
  • Your key can be remembered in your browser. Leave that off on a shared computer.
Check the AI company's data rules

OpenAI and Anthropic each have their own rules for what they keep and for how long. If your images are sensitive, read their current policy for the account type you use before you start.

What does it cost?

The label finder is free. The label reader is charged by the AI company, per picture, so the cost grows with the number of labels. Ways to keep it low:

  • Try 10 labels first. Every step lets you read just the first or a random few.
  • Use the half-price batch option (Batch API) in step 1 and 2 with OpenAI models. Results come back within 24 hours, and you can close the page meanwhile.
  • Set a spending limit in your OpenAI or Anthropic account.
  • Check a random sample in step 3 instead of every label. It is also how you get an error rate you can report.

Words you might meet

We use everyday words first and put the technical term in brackets. These are the ones that come up most.

AI model
The program that reads the pictures. Two families are supported: GPT from OpenAI and Claude from Anthropic (vision-language models).
Key
A long password that lets editha use your AI account and bills it to you (API key). Keep it private.
Spreadsheet
A table you can open in Excel or Google Sheets (CSV file).
Exact transcription
Writing down what the label says, spelling mistakes and old abbreviations included (diplomatic transcription).
Standard columns
Agreed names for biodiversity data, such as eventDate and locality (Darwin Core).
Instructions to the AI
The text sent with each picture that tells the model what to do (prompt).
First, second and third reading
Step 1 and 2 give the first reading (parse 1). Step 3 reads the picture again (parse 2) and a third time where the first two agreed (parse 3).
Teaching the finder
Drawing boxes on a few pictures so the label finder learns what to look for (annotating, then training a model).
Cut out
Save the part of a picture inside a box as its own picture (crop).

Where editha is going

Today, editha has a module for museum specimen labels. Planned next: a module for biodiversity archives such as notebooks and ledgers, and an open-ended module for any other kind of text.
